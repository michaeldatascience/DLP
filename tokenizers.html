Tokenizer Lab

Three algorithms, one corpus, one difference. BPE, WordPiece and Unigram all start from characters and all build subwords — they disagree only about which pair wins. Run them on the lecture's own corpus and watch them diverge on the very first merge.

01

The only thing that actually differs

Everything else — normalise, split on whitespace, build up from characters, stop at a target vocabulary size — is shared. If you hold on to one table from week 2, hold on to this one.

Why dividing changes the answer BPE asks "which pair do I see most often?" WordPiece asks "which pair do I see more often than its two halves would predict?" Dividing by the halves penalises pairs built from already-common characters. An analogy: BPE picks the most-played song; WordPiece picks the song that over-performs relative to how famous its two artists already are. A duet by two unknowns with modest streams beats a duet by two superstars with the same streams.

The slides show a subset — and it matters The lecture's score table lists only the pairs it had already shown for BPE, and among those (a,d) at 0.167 is the winner. Over every pair in the corpus the real winner is (r,y) at 0.333, because y occurs just once and r three times, so the denominator is tiny. Both facts teach the same lesson and the lab shows you the full ranking. What you must carry into the exam is the shape of the rule: rare halves beat common halves at equal pair count. Compare the top row here with the top row on the BPE tab — same corpus, different winner, and that is the entire difference between the two algorithms.

Unigram runs backwards. It starts from a deliberately oversized vocabulary and deletes the subwords it can most afford to lose. Once trained, splitting a word is a search, not a replay of merges: find the segmentation whose probabilities multiply to the largest value. Because multiplying many small numbers underflows, everything is done in logarithms, where multiply becomes add and "largest" means closest to zero.

Why the whole word usually wins Every extra piece multiplies in another probability below 1, so each additional split makes the product smaller. Unigram therefore prefers the segmentation with the fewest pieces that the vocabulary allows. The lecture shows this with knowing: k·now·ing scores 63/4096, know·ing scores 336/4096, and knowing whole scores 768/4096. Fewest pieces, highest probability.

Memory hook Two sculptors and a statistician. BPE and WordPiece add clay — they build up from nothing, greedily, and differ only in what they reach for next. Unigram carves marble — it starts with too much and removes what it will not miss. And only the statistician can read a language with no spaces, because it never needed the spaces to begin with.

Now solve the real questions

Every tokenization question from both past Quiz-1 papers, plus the one from GA2 that the lab reproduces directly.

2026 T1 · Q2 · 2 marks

Consider subword tokenization methods such as BPE and WordPiece as employed in transformer-based language modelling pipelines. Which statement correctly describes their fundamental properties?

(a) They always split words at true morpheme boundaries, e.g. "un-" + "break" + "able".
(b) They learn merge rules from corpus statistics — frequency or likelihood — not token semantics.
(c) They eliminate OOV by ensuring every word is a single token.
(d) They generalise perfectly to out-of-domain text without increasing sequence length.

1
Kill the absolutes first. "always" in (a), "perfectly" in (d). In this paper those words are almost always planted on wrong options.
2
(a) is false on the evidence in front of you: run the BPE tab and the first merges are in then ing — which happen to be morphemes — but the next ones are fragments like th and om that mean nothing. The algorithm counts; it has never heard of morphology.
3
(c) inverts the truth. Subword tokenizers avoid OOV precisely by not keeping every word whole — they fall back to smaller pieces.
4
(d) is false twice over: out-of-domain text fragments into more pieces, so sequence length goes up.
(b) — merge rules come from corpus statistics, never from meaning
2026 T1 · Q3 and 2026 T2 · Q7 · 2 marks each

Two subword tokenizers are trained on the same corpus. Tokenizer A uses a vocabulary of 8,000; Tokenizer B uses 64,000. Which statement most accurately characterises the implications for training efficiency? And its twin: a tokenizer vocabulary is increased from 16K to 128K with the corpus unchanged — what trade-off is most likely?

1
A bigger vocabulary means more merges were performed, so longer chunks exist as single tokens, so any given text needs fewer tokens. Sequences get shorter.
2
But the embedding table is V × d. More rows means more parameters. The table grows.
3
So it is a trade, not a free win — which kills every option claiming both go down, or that total compute simply falls.
4
Both papers wanted the same sentence in different words.
Shorter sequences, larger embedding layer. Length traded against parameters.
2026 T1 · Q4 and 2026 T2 · Q2 · 2 marks each

A subword tokenizer decomposes rare chemical entity names into a large number of fragments, and the model then performs poorly on chemical NER. What is the most principled corrective action? And: a startup builds an LLM for a low-resource agglutinative language; after training a word-level tokenizer with a 30,000 vocabulary, nearly 18% of tokens come out as [UNK]. What most likely improves things while keeping vocabulary growth manageable?

1
Both describe the same failure: the tokenizer's vocabulary was learned on the wrong text, so the input it now faces shatters.
2
Fix the cause, not the symptom. Dropout, batch size, label smoothing and context length are all downstream of a vocabulary that never saw this vocabulary of words — none of them can fuse fragments back together.
3
Case one: retrain the tokenizer on an in-domain chemical corpus, so chemical morphemes become single tokens.
4
Case two: an agglutinative language builds unlimited words by gluing pieces, so a fixed word list can never cover it. Move to subwords — SentencePiece or BPE. Note that raising the minimum frequency threshold would make [UNK] worse, and that disabling normalisation explodes the vocabulary, which is what the question told you to avoid.
Retrain the tokenizer on in-domain text · move from word-level to SentencePiece or BPE
GA2 · Week 2

A BPE tokenizer is trained on 10 million BookCorpus samples at vocabulary sizes 5K, 10K, 15K and 32K, then used to tokenize one fixed sentence. Which statements are correct? Going 5K→10K decreases the token count; 10K→15K leaves it unchanged; 15K→32K decreases it further.

1
All three statements were correct, and the middle one is the interesting one.
2
Token count can only fall or stay flat as vocabulary grows — a larger vocabulary is a strict superset of merges, so no word can ever split into more pieces than before. Any option claiming an increase is wrong by construction.
3
It stays flat over 10K→15K because the merges added in that band are for words the test sentence does not contain. Extra vocabulary only helps if it matches your text.
4
You can watch this on the BPE tab. Run merges past the point where your test word is already whole and its output stops changing while the vocabulary keeps growing.
Monotonically non-increasing — never up, and flat whenever the new merges miss your text
