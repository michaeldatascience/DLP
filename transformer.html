From Corpus to Loss

One sentence of raw text, followed all the way to a number that says how wrong the model was — and then to the gradient that starts backpropagation. This is the front and back of the sandwich. One Block already showed the filling.

01

The whole chain, once

Seven stages. Stage 4 is the transformer block you have already walked through number by number; everything else is on this page.

Answering two questions directly Where does X come from? Stage 4 — a lookup in the embedding table, not the output of pretraining.
How do we know how bad Y is? Stage 7 — the next token in the text is the answer key, and it is free, because the corpus already contains it.
02

Stages 1–2 · a corpus, and a tokenizer learned from it

Everything downstream is determined by this text. The tokenizer is not given to you — it is trained on the corpus before any model training starts.

The corpus

the cat sat on the mat the cat ran the mat sat on the cat

Absurdly small, so every number stays visible. A real one is billions of words. _ marks a word boundary.

Six BPE merges later

The merge rules it discovered

a+t → at  |  at+_ → at_  |  t+h → th  |  th+e → the  |  the+_ → the_  |  c+at_ → cat_

Watch what it learned: the_ and cat_ became single tokens, because they appear often. But ran appears once, so it stayed as loose characters. Frequency alone decided this — the algorithm has no idea what a word is.

The vocabulary

V = 10 tokens, each with a fixed integer id

This list is now frozen for the life of the model. Every piece of text it will ever see must be expressible in these 10 tokens. That is why a tokenizer trained on English shatters when it meets chemistry, and why the fix is to retrain the tokenizer rather than tune the model.

03

Stage 3 · one sentence becomes integers

Take "the cat sat". Replay the six merge rules in order, then look up each resulting piece.

4 tokens from 3 words — "sat" split into two pieces because "sat_" was never merged, while "the_" and "cat_" survived whole.
This is the only place text exists After this line there is no text anywhere in the system. [9, 3, 8, 2] is all the model ever sees. The words are gone.
04

Stage 4 · integers become vectors — this is where X comes from

An integer carries no meaning: id 9 is not "bigger" than id 3. So each id is used as a row number into a table of learned vectors.

The lookup

ids [9, 3, 8, 2] select four rows

Row 9, row 3, row 8, row 2. Stack them, and you have a 4×4 matrix: one row per token, d = 4 numbers wide.

That matrix is the X that One Block starts with. It was never computed by a network — it was looked up.

The embedding table is itself parameters E is 10×4 = 40 learnable numbers, and they are trained exactly like every other weight. Initially the rows are random, so "cat_" and "mat_" mean nothing and sit nowhere in particular. Pretraining is what drags similar tokens near each other.

In general that table is V × d — the formula from Transformer Anatomy, now with a reason attached.
X being square here is an accident — do not read anything into it This sentence happens to tokenize into 4 pieces, and I happened to choose d = 4. T and d are completely unrelated quantities. T is how many tokens are in this particular input; d is a fixed property of the model. Real shapes look like 512×768 or 2048×4096.

Two more things X is not. It is not a subset of E — it is a selection with repetition. Tokenize "the the the" and X is 3×d holding row 9 three times over. And T can exceed V: a thousand-token passage drawn from a ten-token vocabulary gives a 1000×4 X.
05

Stage 5 · the blockalready covered

X goes in, attention lets the tokens see each other, the feed-forward network thinks about each position, and a 4×4 matrix comes out. Every number of this is in One Block, End to End — nothing new here.

In 4 x 4, out 4 x 4. Stack 32 of these and you have a 7B model.
06

Stage 6 · from width 4 back out to the whole vocabulary

Here is the exact problem. After the block, each token is described by 4 numbers. But the question is "which of the 10 vocabulary entries comes next?" — so we need 10 answers, one per candidate. Four numbers have to become ten scores.

The idea

Give every vocabulary entry its own detector

W_lm has shape d × V = 4×10. Read it by column: there are ten columns, one per vocabulary entry, and each column is a list of 4 numbers.

The score for token j is the dot product of the block's output vector with column j. Column j acts as a detector that asks: does this output vector look like the kind of thing that comes before token j? Ten columns, ten questions, ten scores.

Those ten numbers are row 0 of the logits matrix below. Four rows of this = the whole thing.
Logits

One raw score per vocabulary entry, per position

Logits are unnormalised scores — any real number, positive or negative, with no requirement to sum to anything. Row 0 is the model's opinion about what follows token 0. Here s scores highest at 1.19 and the_ lowest at −0.76, but the weights are random, so this is noise, not knowledge.

Softmax

Scores become probabilities

Exponentiate everything, then divide by the row's total. Now every entry is between 0 and 1 and each row sums to exactly 1.

Weight tying — and the intuition it hands you W_lm is 4×10 and the embedding table E is 10×4. Same shape, transposed. Many models simply reuse E as the output layer, which is called weight tying, and it is why GPT-2's 124M needs no separate output matrix.

When they are tied, the score for token j becomes dot(output vector, E[j]) — literally "which token's embedding does my output vector most resemble?" The model answers by pointing its output in the direction of the token it wants, and the scores fall out as similarities. That is the cleanest way to hold Stage 6 in your head, even for models that do not tie.
07

Stage 7 · how wrong was it?

Here is the idea that makes pretraining possible without a single human label: the answer key is the text itself. At each position, the correct next token is simply the token that actually came next.

PositionInput tokenMust predictProbability it gaveLoss = −ln(p)
2.190 mean loss across the four positions
Check this number against chance With V = 10 tokens and no knowledge at all, the model would spread probability evenly — 0.1 each — giving a loss of −ln(0.1) = 2.303.

We got 2.190. The weights are random, so that is pure luck, and it tells you the model knows nothing. An untrained model's loss is always about ln(V). That is the baseline every training run starts from, and watching the loss fall below it is watching the model learn.
Why the logarithm

−ln(p) turns "probability" into "surprise"

If the model gave the right token probability 1, the loss is −ln(1) = 0 — no surprise, no error. As p falls toward 0, −ln(p) climbs without limit. Confident and wrong is punished far harder than uncertain and wrong.

08

The gradient that starts everything

Now differentiate the loss with respect to the logits. For softmax with cross-entropy this collapses to something startlingly simple.

dLogits = (probabilities − one-hot target) / T
Read the signs

One negative per row, everything else positive

At the target column the value is (p − 1), which is negative — gradient descent subtracts it, so that logit goes up.

At every other column the value is (p − 0), positive — those logits go down.

Each step nudges the right answer up and all 9 wrong answers down, by exactly how much probability they were each wrongly given. Do that a few trillion times and you have a pretrained model.

This is the dY from One Block In One Block, step 4 began with "dY arrives from above". This is where it arrives from. The matrix above travels back through W_lm, then through the block, producing dW2 and dW1 and all the rest exactly as you watched.

The chain is now closed in both directions: corpus → tokenizer → ids → X → block → logits → loss, and then the gradient all the way back.
09

So what is pretraining, exactly?

Everything above, for one sentence, is one training step. Pretraining is that loop run until the loss stops falling.

StageDone how oftenProduces
Train the tokenizerOnce, before anythingMerge rules and a frozen vocabulary
Tokenize the corpusOnceOne long stream of integer ids

Continued pre-trainingOptional, afterSame loop, same loss, new domain corpus
Instruction tuningOptional, afterSame machinery, but the corpus is instruction-response pairs and the loss is concentrated on the responses

Why they are all "the same thing with a different corpus" Nothing about the machinery changes between pretraining and continued pre-training — same next-token loss, same backward pass, different text. Instruction tuning changes what the loss is computed over, not how. And LoRA changes only which weights receive the gradient.

This is why the exam asks whether continued pre-training and instruction tuning share an objective. They share the mechanism, which is why it feels like yes. They differ in the objective, because one scores every token of plain text and the other scores only the response to an instruction.
DA5013 · Weeks 3 & 4

One Block, End to End

Three tokens, four dimensions, every number visible. The same three tokens go through the feed-forward network, back through it, then through attention with two heads. Nothing is abstract and nothing is skipped.

01

The setup, and the colour code

A real transformer has d = 768 or more. This one has d = 4, so every matrix fits on screen. The structure is identical — only the numbers are small. Three tokens, four dimensions each.

Three kinds of thing appear on this page, and telling them apart is the whole lesson. The colours never change meaning.

WEIGHTLearned. Persists forever. One entry per parameter. These are what "7B parameters" counts.
ACTIVATIONComputed fresh each forward pass, thrown away after the backward pass. One entry per token per dimension.
GRADIENTProduced by the backward pass. Always the same shape as the weight it belongs to. Discarded after the update.
The one question that classifies everything Does this thing have one number per parameter, or one number per token? Weights and gradients are per parameter. Activations are per token. That single distinction explains why activations grow when you raise the batch size and why the model's memory does not.
02

Feed-forward, going forwards

The feed-forward network widens each token's vector, applies a non-linearity, and squeezes it back. Here it widens 4 → 8. A real one widens 4× (768 → 3072); I use 2× so the matrices fit. Each token is processed independently — no token looks at any other in this part.

Step 1

Widen: multiply by the first weight matrix

Each of the 3 token rows is multiplied by W1. A row of 4 numbers meets a 4×8 matrix and comes out as 8 numbers.

Step 2

Non-linearity: ReLU keeps the positives

ReLU(x) = max(0, x). Negative entries become zero. This is where the network gains the ability to express something other than a straight line. Real GPT uses GELU, a smoothed version of exactly this shape.

Notice the zeros. They matter in a moment — a zero here blocks a gradient later.

Step 3

Squeeze: back down to width 4

W2 takes the 8 numbers back to 4, so the block's output has the same shape as its input. That is what lets you stack blocks.

Count what is on screen Weights: W1 is 4×8 = 32 and W2 is 8×4 = 32, so 64 parameters. In the general case that is 2 · d · 4d = 8d² — the "feed-forward is eight" from Transformer Anatomy.

Activations: X 12, H 24, A 24 = 60 numbers. Add a fourth token and the weights do not change at all, but the activations grow to 80.
03

Feed-forward, going backwards

Something upstream tells us how wrong Y was. That arrives as dY, one number per entry of Y — "nudge this entry down, that one up". The job now is to turn that into a correction for every weight.

Step 4

The gradient of W2 — and why A had to be kept

dW2 = Aᵀ @ dY. Read that carefully: computing how to correct W2 requires A, the activation from step 2.

This is the entire reason activations cost memory. They are not stored because someone chose to cache them. They are stored because the backward pass cannot run without them. Throw A away and dW2 is uncomputable.

Step 5

Push the signal back through, then the gradient of W1

Three small steps: dA = dY @ W2ᵀ sends the signal back across W2; dH = dA ⊙ (H > 0) passes it through the ReLU, zeroing wherever the forward pass zeroed; then dW1 = Xᵀ @ dH.

That middle step is why the zeros mattered. A unit switched off in the forward pass receives no gradient in the backward pass.

The shape fact to carry away W1 is 4×8 and dW1 is 4×8. W2 is 8×4 and dW2 is 8×4. A gradient always has exactly the shape of the weight it corrects.

So "gradients cost the same as weights" is not a coincidence or an approximation — it is forced by the arithmetic. And momentum and variance are also one-per-parameter, because they are running averages of these gradients. That is where sixteen bytes per parameter comes from, and you have now watched it happen.
04

Attention, on the same three tokens

The feed-forward network never let tokens see each other. Attention is the part that does. Same X as before, same four dimensions.

Step 6

Three projections of the same input

X is multiplied by three separate weight matrices to produce three different views of the same tokens. The names are a filing-cabinet metaphor: Query is what this token is looking for, Key is what each token advertises about itself, Value is what it actually hands over if chosen.

All three are 4×4 here, so Q, K and V each come out 3×4 — one row per token, exactly like X.

Step 7

Split into two heads — and notice no parameters are added

Two heads means: cut each of Q, K, V down the middle. Head 0 takes columns 0–1, head 1 takes columns 2–3. Each head gets width d/h = 2.

Nothing new is created. The same 4×4 matrices are being read in two halves. This is why the heads count is a decoy in parameter questions — doubling the heads just slices the same numbers more finely.

Step 8

Each head: score, mask, softmax

scores = Qₕ @ Kₕᵀ / √2 gives a 3×3 grid — how much each token wants to attend to each other token. The √2 is √(head width) and is only a normaliser, keeping the numbers in a range where softmax behaves.

Then the causal mask sets everything above the diagonal to −∞, so no token sees the future, and softmax turns each row into probabilities summing to 1.

Look at the first row after softmax: it is always 1, 0, 0. Token 1 has no past, so it can only attend to itself. And the score grid is 3×3 — T×T — which is the O(T²) term.

Step 9

Mix the values, stitch the heads, project out

Each head computes P @ Vₕ — a weighted blend of value rows, using the attention probabilities as the weights. The two heads' outputs are concatenated back to width 4, then passed through a fourth matrix W_O.

The whole block, counted Attention: four 4×4 matrices = 64 parameters, which is 4d². Feed-forward: 64, which is 8d² at this reduced width (2d instead of 4d). At real proportions the FFN is twice the attention, giving 12d² per block.

Every number in Q, K, V, the score grids and the outputs is an activation — gone after the backward pass. Only the four matrices persist.

DA5013 · Week 3

Transformer Anatomy

Where every parameter in a transformer physically lives, and how to count them without memorising anything. Change the dimensions below and watch the model's size assemble itself. Four past-paper questions are solved at the bottom, then you're quizzed.

01

A transformer keeps its parameters in three places

That is the whole idea. Every parameter-counting question in this course is just asking you to add up three buckets. Before the formulas: parameter means one learnable number — a single cell inside a matrix. When a model is called "7B", that is a count of these cells.

Place 1 · once per model

The lookup tables

V·d + ctx·d

One row per vocabulary entry, so the model can turn a token id into a vector. Plus one row per position, so it knows where in the sentence each token sits. Both rows are d numbers wide.

Place 2 · once per block

Attention projections

4 · d²

Four square matrices, each d×d: query, key, value, and the output projection that mixes the heads back together. Four squares, hence 4d².

Place 3 · once per block

Feed-forward network

2 · d · 4d = 8 · d²

Widens each vector from d to 4d, then squeezes it back to d. Two matrices of d×4d. Twice the size of the attention beside it.

The sentence that replaces the formulas "Attention is four, feed-forward is eight, so twelve per block." Add the two lookup tables once, and you can rebuild any model's size from its config. That is the entire skill.

03

Three decoys the paper plants

Each of these appears in a question specifically to be used, and in every case the right move is to ignore it. Verify each one on the panel above.

Decoy 1 · the number of heads Drag the heads slider from 1 to 64. The total does not move. Heads split the existing d×d matrices into h slices of width d/h — they do not add matrices. Twelve heads of width 64 is exactly 12 × 64 = 768 numbers wide, which is the same single d×d matrix.

T1 Q19 gave you "12 attention heads" and the answer was 4d², using d alone.
Decoy 2 · the sequence length T T is how many tokens are in this particular input. Parameters are the model's permanent furniture; they exist before any input arrives and do not change when a different sentence shows up. T affects how much activation memory a forward pass needs, and it drives the O(T²) cost of attention — but it never appears in a parameter count.

T1 Q19 opened with "a very short sequence of only 3 tokens". It was irrelevant.
Decoy 3 · the context length This one is subtler, because ctx does appear — but only in the smallest bucket. Set d to 2048 and drag ctx from 2048 to 8192: the total barely moves, because you are only adding rows to the position table while the blocks stay untouched.

GA4 asked how the memory to load a 7B model changes when context goes 2048 → 8000. Answer: by about 12.2M parameters, roughly 0.05 GB on a 28 GB model. It stays 28 GB.

01

The single most useful sentence about decoder-only models A decoder-only model is not "half a transformer". It is the decoder with the cross-attention layer removed, because cross-attention exists only to read an encoder's output and there is no encoder to read. That is why GPT's block has two sublayers — masked attention and feed-forward — while the full decoder has three. Select Decoder-only above and the cross-attention block is the one that greys out inside an otherwise intact column.
02

What actually changes: the mask

The families share their machinery almost exactly. What really separates them is which cells of the attention matrix are allowed. A cell holds 0 where attention is permitted and −∞ where it is blocked, because −∞ becomes 0 after softmax and so contributes nothing.

Rows are queries — the token doing the looking. Columns are keys — the token being looked at. Cell (i,j) asks: may token i attend to token j?

The off-by-one that 2026 T2 made a 3-mark question 
causal_mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(seq_len, seq_len), diagonal=0).bool()
masked_scores = scores.masked_fill(causal_mask, float("-inf"))
attn_weights  = F.softmax(masked_scores, dim=-1)

diagonal=0 includes the main diagonal, so every token is forbidden from attending to itself. Row 0 then has nothing left to look at — it is entirely −∞ — and softmax over a row of all −∞ gives NaN. Set the selector above to 0 and watch the first row turn solid red. The fix is diagonal=1, which blocks only what is strictly above the diagonal.

Why the course calls the mask "strictly upper triangular" The blocked cells — the −∞ entries — sit strictly above the main diagonal. The deck writes it out as a 4×4 matrix with zeros on and below the diagonal and −∞ above. A past paper marked "the attention mask is strictly upper triangular" as a correct statement, so the wording matters.

2026 T2 · Q9 · 3 marks

The following code is intended to implement causal masking for self-attention, where each token can attend only to itself and previous tokens. seq_len = 4, causal_mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(seq_len, seq_len), diagonal=0).bool(), then masked_fill(causal_mask, float("-inf")) and a softmax over the last dimension. What is the effect of running this code as written?

1
torch.triu(M, diagonal=k) keeps the upper triangle starting k positions from the main diagonal. At k=0 the main diagonal is included.
2
Those kept entries are what gets filled with −∞. So cell (i,i) — a token attending to itself — is blocked, which is not what causal masking means. Causal allows self-attention; it blocks only the future.
3
Row 0 has no past at all, and now loses itself too, so every cell in row 0 is −∞.
4
Softmax over a row of all −∞ divides zero by zero and produces NaN. Switch the panel above to diagonal=0 to see the row turn solid red.
5
Reject the two tempting distractors: it raises no RuntimeError — triu and masked_fill compose perfectly legally — and the weights do not "sum to more than 1", because softmax always normalises to 1 wherever it is defined at all.
The current token is masked too; row 0 becomes entirely −∞, which can yield NaN weights

Which statements correctly distinguish different Transformer architectures and their typical training objectives? (a) Encoder-only models are commonly trained with Masked Language Modelling objectives. (b) Decoder-only models rely on causal masking and autoregressive inference. (c) Encoder-decoder models are well suited for sequence-to-sequence tasks. (d) All Transformer architectures use identical attention masks.

1
(a), (b) and (c) are each the textbook pairing of a family with its objective. All three correct.
2
(d) is the odd one out, and it is false for the exact reason this page exists: the mask is the one thing that genuinely differs. An encoder has none; a decoder has a strictly upper triangular one.
3
Pattern worth noting: in a four-option multiple-select on this paper, three options were correct and the fourth contained the word "identical". Absolutes stay a reliable tell.
(a), (b), (c) — not (d)
2026 T1 · Q16 · 3 marks · multiple select

A model is trained using the Causal Language Modelling objective with a standard cross-entropy loss. Which statements correctly describe the training dynamics? (a) The loss at position t depends only on tokens before t. (b) The attention mask is strictly upper triangular. (c) Future tokens contribute gradients to earlier positions. (d) The joint probability of the sequence is factorised autoregressively.

1
(a) True. That is the definition of causal: the prediction at t is conditioned on x₁…x₍t₋₁₎ and nothing later.
2
(b) True, in the deck's own words — the −∞ entries lie strictly above the diagonal.
3
(c) False, and this is the one that catches people. Gradients flow backwards through the computation graph, but the mask severed the forward connection from future tokens to earlier positions. No forward path means no gradient path. A token that was never read cannot be blamed for the error.
4
(d) True: P(x₁…x_T) = ∏ P(x_t | x₁…x₍t₋₁₎), the chain rule written out, which is the whole content of the CLM objective.
(a), (b), (d) — not (c)
2026 T1 · Q6 and Q7 · 2 marks each

(a) A loop builds AutoModelForMaskedLM.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased") and calls it with labels=batch_ids on a large domain corpus with no labelled task targets, then backpropagates. What is the most precise interpretation? (b) In the standard Transformer, which component scales quadratically, O(N²), with input sequence length N?

1
(a) Three signals decide it. The class is ForMaskedLM, so the objective is MLM. The corpus is unlabelled and domain-specific. The weights start from a pretrained checkpoint rather than random.
2
Pretrained start + original objective + new domain = continual pre-training. Passing labels=input_ids is not supervision; it is how the MLM collator scores the model on reconstructing its own masked input.
3
So it is not instruction tuning — there are no instructions — and no classifier head is being trained.
4
(b) The attention score matrix QKᵀ is N×N, one entry per pair of positions, so it grows with the square of the length. Embedding lookups are O(N), residual additions are O(N), and the feed-forward layers act position by position, also O(N).
5
Note this is the one place sequence length matters. It drives compute and activation memory — never the parameter count.
