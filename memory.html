DA5013 · Week 4

The Memory Ledger

Training holds four copies of your model, not one. Once you can name the four, every memory question in week 4 becomes arithmetic, and every technique in the syllabus becomes a line item that strikes one of them out. This is the single largest block of marks on the paper.

01

Four copies, four bytes each

A parameter is one learnable number. To run a model you need the number itself. To train it you also need somewhere to accumulate its error, and AdamW keeps two running averages besides. Four numbers per parameter, four bytes each in fp32.

Copy 1 · always

The weights

4 bytes × N

The model itself. This is the only copy inference needs, which is why running a model costs a quarter of training it.

Copy 2 · training only

The gradients

4 bytes × N

One number per parameter saying which way to move it. Created by the backward pass, discarded after the step.

Copy 3 · AdamW

Momentum

4 bytes × N

A running average of recent gradients, so the update keeps rolling in a consistent direction instead of lurching with each batch.

Copy 4 · AdamW

Variance

4 bytes × N

A running average of squared gradients, letting each parameter get its own step size. Together with momentum these are the "optimizer states".

The sentence that answers every memory question Four copies, four bytes each, so sixteen bytes a parameter. A billion parameters costs 16 GB to train and 4 GB to load. The course defines 1 GB as 10⁹ bytes, so you never convert anything — just multiply and move the decimal point.
An analogy worth keeping Think of moving house. The weights are your furniture. The gradients are the note on each item saying which room it goes to. Momentum is your memory of which way you have been carrying things, and variance is your sense of which items are fragile and need small, careful moves. Only the furniture has to exist for you to live in the house — the other three are the apparatus of moving it, and you can put them down once you have arrived.

DA5013 · Week 4

The Five Levers

Mixed precision, gradient accumulation, gradient checkpointing, quantization, LoRA. Each one taken apart separately, each answering the same four questions, so they stop being a list of names and become five machines you can tell apart.

01

Everything lands in one of two piles

Before any technique: GPU memory during training splits into exactly two kinds of thing, and no lever attacks both. Get this and the rest is bookkeeping.

Pile A · per parameterWeights, gradients, momentum, variance. Four numbers for every parameter in the model. Grows with model size. Completely unaffected by batch size.
Pile B · per tokenActivations — every intermediate value the forward pass produced and the backward pass still needs. Grows with batch size and sequence length. Unaffected by how many parameters you have.
The sorting rule Levers 1, 2 and 3 attack Pile B. Mixed precision, gradient accumulation, gradient checkpointing.
Levers 4 and 5 attack Pile A. Quantization, LoRA.

That is the whole lever grid, and it is why "which does mixed precision shrink?" has an answer that surprises people.

fp16 vs bf16

Range matters more than precision

fp16 spends its bits on precision and ends up with only 5 exponent bits, so its largest value is 65,504 and small gradients around 1e-8 underflow to zero. Training with it needs a trick called loss scaling: multiply the loss by 1024 before the backward pass so gradients land in representable range, then divide out afterwards.

bf16 keeps fp32's full 8 exponent bits, so it has the same range as fp32 with fewer digits of precision. Nothing underflows and no loss scaling is needed. Networks turn out to care far more about range than precision, which is why modern training is nearly all bf16.

Why the fp32 master copy is not optional — watch a weight fail to move

Take a weight of 0.5 and an update of 1e-4 (learning rate 1e-4, gradient 1.0). The gap between representable numbers near 0.5 is:

fp320.00000006far smaller than the update
fp160.00048828larger than the update itself

So in fp16, 0.5 − 0.0001 rounds straight back to 0.5. The weight does not move. I ran 2000 consecutive updates in each precision:

fp32, after 2000 steps0.3000moved, as it should
fp16, after 2000 steps0.5000never moved once

Two thousand updates, zero progress. That is what the fp32 master copy prevents. The 16-bit copy does the fast matrix multiplications; the 32-bit copy is where the optimizer actually accumulates change. Delete it to "save memory" and training silently stops working.

03

Gradient accumulation

attacks pile B
The problemYou want batch 64 for stable gradients. Batch 64's activations do not fit.
What it doesRuns 4 passes of batch 16, adds the gradients into one buffer, updates once.
What it costsTime. Four sequential passes instead of one parallel one. Same total arithmetic, less parallelism.
What it does NOT doIt does not reduce optimizer states or gradient storage. One buffer, not four.
Why you want a big batch at all

A gradient from 4 samples is noisy; from 64 it is not

Each sample pulls the weights in a slightly different direction. Averaging more samples cancels the disagreement and leaves the signal, so the step you take is more likely to be the right one. Tiny batches make training jittery and sometimes unstable.

Accumulation buys you that statistical benefit without ever holding a big batch in memory.

The two statements the exam plants

"Gradient accumulation reduces the number of optimizer states." False. Momentum and variance are one per parameter regardless of how many passes you summed first. The optimizer has no idea accumulation happened.

"The optimizer updates after every batch." False when accumulation is on. With gradient_accumulation_steps = 8 it updates after every eight passes. That is the definition of the setting.

Analogy Carrying a week's shopping in four trips instead of one. The same goods arrive, your arms carry a quarter as much at any moment, and it takes four times as long. What does not change is the size of the kitchen you unload into — that is the gradient buffer.
04

Gradient checkpointing

attacks pile B
The problemThe backward pass needs values the forward pass computed, so by default everything is kept.
What it doesKeeps only each layer's input. Rebuilds the rest by re-running that layer when needed.
What it costsAbout +33% compute — roughly one extra forward pass over the network.
What it does NOT doTouches nothing in Pile A. Weights, gradients and optimizer states are unchanged.
The reason activations are stored at all

You saw this in the Transformer Workshop

dW2 = Aᵀ @ dY. Computing the gradient of W2 requires A, the activation from the forward pass. Activations are not cached as an optimisation — the backward pass is arithmetically impossible without them.

Checkpointing does not dispute this. It just says: don't store it, recompute it at the moment you need it.

Without checkpointing

849.3M numbers  =  1.70 GB

With checkpointing

89.7M numbers  =  0.18 GB
9.5× less memory for 33% more time Twelve layers, batch 2, sequence 1024, bf16. Without: every layer's full internals, 70.8M numbers each. With: twelve small layer inputs (18.9M total) plus one layer's worth of scratch space while it is being rebuilt (70.8M).

When to reach for it: when activations dominate — long context, deep model, large batch — and you have compute to spare. If your problem is the optimizer rather than activations, it buys you nothing.
Analogy Instead of keeping every line of working, you keep only the answer at the bottom of each page. If the examiner questions line 43, you redo that one page. Much less paper, a bit more pencil.
05

Quantization

attacks pile A
The problemA 7B model in fp32 is 28 GB before training has even started.
What it doesStores each weight as a small integer plus one shared scale factor per block.
What it costsAccuracy — small, but real. The weights are approximations now.
What it does NOT doNothing for activations, and it is normally used on frozen weights only.
The mechanism

It is not simply "fewer bits" — there is a scale factor

An int8 can hold −127 to 127. Weights are small decimals. So you find the largest magnitude in a block, divide by 127 to get a scale, and store each weight as the nearest integer multiple of that scale.

w1w2w3w4w5w6w7w8
original fp320.076-0.209-0.165-0.9770.7200.458-0.1300.310
stored int810-27-21-1279460-1740
reconstructed0.077-0.208-0.162-0.9770.7230.462-0.1310.308
scale = max|w| / 127 = 0.00769  ·  largest error 0.0036  ·  memory 32 bytes → 8 bytes + 4 for the scale

Note the largest weight (−0.977) maps to exactly −127 and comes back perfectly. Everything else is approximated, and the error is bounded by half a scale step.

DA5013 · Week 4

LoRA on a real matrix

One 4×4 matrix, frozen, with two thin matrices learned beside it. Every number visible, then the same idea scaled up to a real model with the sliders.

01

Now LoRA, on W_Q

W_Q is the 4×4 query matrix from the Transformer Workshop — the one that turned the token vectors X into the queries Q. Fine-tuning normally means computing a gradient for all 16 of its numbers and keeping momentum and variance for each. LoRA says: don't.

Step 10

Freeze the matrix; learn a correction beside it

W_Q is frozen — it still runs in every forward pass, it still sits in GPU memory, but no gradient is ever computed for it. Instead two thin matrices are added: A of shape 4×1 and B of shape 1×4. Their product A @ B is 4×4 — the same shape as W_Q.

The model now uses W_Q + A@B wherever it used W_Q. The 1 in those shapes is the rank r.

Why B starts at zero A@B is all zeros at step 0, so W_Q + A@B equals W_Q exactly. The adapted model starts out identical to the pretrained one and departs from it only as training proceeds. Initialising both to random would corrupt the pretrained behaviour on the very first forward pass.
Step 11

After a few steps, B has moved

Only A and B received gradients, so only they changed. Their product is now a non-zero 4×4 correction, which is added to the frozen matrix to give the effective one.

The toy does not show the saving — the formula does Here: 8 trainable against 16 frozen. That is 50%, which is no saving at all. That is not a flaw in the example; it is the point. The ratio is

2·d·r / d² = 2r / d

At d = 4, r = 1 that is 0.5. The win appears only when d is large and r stays small — and r stays small because the correction a fine-tune needs is simple even when the matrix is huge. Move the sliders.
— of one matrix is trainable  ·  2r/d
Head-only fine-tuning, for contrast LoRA touches every layer with a small correction. Head-only touches one new layer and freezes everything else. Its size is just d × num_labels — for d = 2048 and 8 entity labels that is 16,384 parameters, roughly 0.0002% of a 7B model. Hundreds of times smaller than LoRA, and correspondingly less able to adapt: the body cannot change at all, so it only works when the pretrained features already suit your task.

The three statements that are always wrong

"FP16 automatically reduces optimizer state size by half." It does not. Mixed precision keeps a full fp32 master copy of the weights and the optimizer moments, because repeatedly adding tiny updates to a 16-bit number loses them to rounding. That master copy is the entire point. What fp16 halves is activations and gradient storage, not optimizer state.

"Gradient accumulation reduces the number of optimizer states." It does not. The optimizer still keeps two numbers per parameter no matter how many forward passes you sum before stepping. Accumulation changes when you step, never what you store.

"Optimizer updates occur after every batch." With gradient_accumulation_steps = 8 they occur after every eight. That is the definition.

Memory hook for the levers Shrink, Skip, Forget, Freeze. Quantization shrinks each number. Accumulation skips the update until later. Checkpointing forgets activations and recomputes them. LoRA freezes the base so no gradients are owed on it. Four verbs, four different victims — and mixed precision is the odd one out, which is exactly why it is the one the exam lies about.

2026 T1 · Q13 and 2026 T2 · Q10 · 3 marks each · multiple select

Given TrainingArguments(per_device_train_batch_size=1, gradient_accumulation_steps=8, fp16=True, max_grad_norm=1.0), which effects are expected? And in T2's variant with fp16=True, gradient_accumulation_steps=8, per_device_train_batch_size=2, which statements are correct?

1
Effective batch size = per-device batch × accumulation steps. 1×8 = 8, and 2×8 = 16. Both papers asked you to confirm it exceeds the per-device figure. Correct.
2
fp16 reduces memory against fp32 training, via activations and gradient storage. Correct.
3
max_grad_norm=1.0 is gradient clipping: it caps the update size so one freak batch cannot wreck the weights. Correct.
4
"Gradient accumulation reduces the number of optimizer states." False. Two states per parameter regardless.
5
"Optimizer updates occur after every batch." False. After every eight.
6
"FP16 automatically reduces optimizer state size by half." False. The fp32 master copy is retained on purpose.
Effective batch grows · memory falls · clipping limits updates · accumulation can simulate larger batches. Everything about optimizer-state size is false.
2026 T2 · Q12 · 3 marks · and 2026 T1 · Q11 · 2 marks

(a) A team hits GPU out-of-memory errors while fine-tuning a language model. Which actions may help: mixed precision, quantization, gradient checkpointing, LoRA, increasing vocabulary size? (b) Which single technique is most effective at reducing GPU memory during training without modifying the model architecture: gradient clipping, mixed-precision training, increasing learning-rate warmup, or saving checkpoints less often?

1
(a) The first four each attack a real line in the ledger — activations, weights, activations again, and gradients-plus-optimizer. All four help.
2
Increasing vocabulary size makes the embedding table larger. It is the only option that moves memory the wrong way, and it is attractive because the other four are all "advanced technique" words.
3
(b) Gradient clipping bounds the gradient's magnitude, not its storage — the tensor is the same size either way. Warmup is a learning-rate schedule and saves nothing. Checkpoint frequency is disk, not VRAM.
4
Only mixed precision touches GPU memory at all, and it does so without changing a single layer.
(a) all but increasing vocabulary size  ·  (b) mixed-precision training
GA4 · Week 4 · the three you should re-derive

A 7B model, context 2048, embedding dimension 2048, dataset of 1,000,000 samples each exactly one context long, per-device batch 16, gradient accumulation 4. (a) How many optimizer update steps complete one epoch on a single GPU? (b) Fine-tuning for NER with 8 labels, tuning only the classification head — how many parameters in that head, excluding bias? (c) Does raising LoRA's rank r increase the number of tunable parameters?

1
(a) One update consumes an effective batch of 16 × 4 = 64 samples. 1,000,000 ÷ 64 = 15,625 steps.
2
The companion True/False — "the weight update happens only at the end of one epoch" — is false, and these 15,625 steps are the proof.
3
(b) The head maps the d-dimensional output vector to one score per label: 2048 × 8 = 16,384. The question says to exclude the bias, which would have added 8.
4
(c) True. LoRA replaces a d×d update with two matrices of shape d×r and r×d, so the trainable count is about 2dr per adapted matrix — linear in r. Rank is the dial that trades capacity against memory.
15,625 steps · 16,384 head parameters · rank up means parameters up
